Two machines for getting words you did not choose

The Wheel
& the Scissors

Georg Philipp Harsdörffer built a paper computer for the German language and told the bookbinder to cut it out. Three centuries later, in a hotel room on the rue Gît-le-Cœur, the scissors came back — pointed the other way.

The plate carries 48 prefixes, 50 initial letters and rhyming letters, 12 medial letters, 120 final letters and 24 suffixes — Harsdörffer's own caption. Multiplied out that is roughly 83 million words; counts of around 100 million circulate too, because some scholars read the second ring as holding 60 entries rather than 50. Either way the claim in the heading — the entire German language on one leaf — is a claim about coverage, not about sense.

He was candid about the gap. The rings, he wrote, are chiefly useful for finding rhymes: you hunt the rhyming syllable on the third and fourth rings and turn the second ring against it. Whether a compound the wheel produces actually means anything, the user has to decide. The device does the enumerating; it does not do the judging. That division of labour is the whole idea, and it is the same one Llull's rings make three centuries earlier.

The rings above are a working rebuild, not a facsimile: 24 prefixes, 24 openings, the 12 medials Harsdörffer specifies, 24 closings, 24 suffixes, chosen so the mechanism runs. His actual inventory of 254 parts is on the plate in Nuremberg. What survives here is the operation — and the fact that a good third of what comes out is a real word, another third is a word the language would accept if it needed one, and the rest is noise.

✂

Notice where the Denckring begins: with an instruction to take a knife to the page. Harsdörffer cuts paper into parts in order to assemble words. In 1959 Brion Gysin cut through a stack of newspapers while trimming a mount, saw the sliced layers line up into sentences, and ran the same operation backwards — cutting finished language apart to see what it had been hiding.

Gysin's claim for the method was that writing was fifty years behind painting: collage had been
      ordinary in the studio since 1912 and unthinkable on the page. William Burroughs took the technique
      into the Nova trilogy and pushed it further with the fold-in, laying a folded page against another so
      that two texts meet along a vertical seam. Tzara had proposed something adjacent in 1920 — cut an
      article into single words, shake them in a bag, draw them out in order — but as provocation rather
      than working method.

    
What the cut does is break the sentence's forward pressure. Prose reads as a chain of commitments,
      each clause narrowing what the next one can be; slicing across the column snaps those commitments and
      leaves phrases adjacent that no author would have put together. Most results are rubbish. The value
      lies in the small proportion that isn't, and finding it costs exactly the labour that the Denckring's
      user spent deciding whether a compound meant anything.

    
Both machines make the same bet: that a mechanical operation on language can turn up something the
      operator could not have reached by intending it, and that the operator remains responsible for
      recognising which of the results is worth keeping. Harsdörffer turns rings and gets candidates.
      Gysin cuts pages and gets candidates. Neither device ever produced a judgement, and neither pretended
      to.
