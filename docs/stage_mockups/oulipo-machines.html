Two Machines
from the Ouvroir

Queneau and Le Lionnais set up a workshop to build constraints rather than poems — devices that hold a shape open and let anybody run material through them. Two of the first are below, both from 1961. One assembles; the other dismantles. Neither needs an author present to keep working.

I

Cent mille milliards de poèmes Queneau, 1961

Ten sonnets, each printed on a card cut into fourteen strips. Every sonnet keeps the same rhyme scheme and the same rhyme sounds, so line 6 of any sonnet will serve as line 6 of any other. Flip the strips and the book holds a hundred million million poems. Queneau said the model was not the surrealists' exquisite corpse but a children's flip-book of interchangeable heads.

A sort of machine for manufacturing poems — Queneau on his own book, adding that the supply, though finite, runs to nearly two hundred million years of reading around the clock.
Raymond Queneau, jacket note, Gallimard 1961

The arithmetic is only half the trick. The hard part is writing 140 lines that are grammatically self-sufficient — no clause may reach across a strip, because the next strip is not going to be there. Every line has to end its own sentence or sit as an apposition that survives any neighbour.

The rhymes need a second constraint that is easy to miss: no rhyme word may appear at two different positions in the sonnet. Otherwise a flip eventually lands the same word twice in one poem and the whole illusion of a written object collapses. The ten sonnets here are mine, since Queneau's are still in copyright; the scheme is his — ABAB ABAB CCD EED, one rhyme sound per letter throughout, and four disjoint word-pools for the four A positions so that no combination can ever repeat itself.

II

N+7 Lescure, 1961

Jean Lescure's rule: take a text, take a dictionary, and replace every noun with the seventh noun that follows it. Nothing else changes — not the grammar, not the articles, not the line breaks. The result keeps the exact skeleton of the original and loses all of its subject matter, which is a fairly precise demonstration of how much of a sentence's meaning is carried by things that are not its nouns.

N +7

The dictionary is not a neutral part of the apparatus — it is the other half of the constraint. A Larousse throws up specialist vocabulary and archaisms; a schoolroom word-list produces something tamer and stranger. This one holds a few hundred ordinary nouns, so the substitutions stay recognisable and the joke lands closer to home. Change the offset and the same text yields a different poem; N+7 is a family of transformations, not one.

Both machines make the same wager as Llull's wheel and Harsdörffer's discs three and six centuries earlier: that a rule applied mechanically to language will turn up something the operator could not have intended, and that the operator still has to decide which results are worth keeping. What the Oulipo added was candour about it. The constraint is published alongside the work, so the reader can run the machine too — which is the only reason this page can exist.
