Which figure this is

Three concentric circles, each carrying the nine letters B to K, J skipped. In the print the outer circle is fixed and the two inner ones turn, cut out and fastened at the centre with a knotted thread — a logical machine made of paper, two centuries before Harsdörffer sent his own leaf to the bookbinder. Every setting of the wheels shows a chamber of three letters.

The scene departs from the print in one way, and it is worth naming rather than leaving for somebody to notice: all three of its wheels turn. The set of chambers this reaches is exactly the set a fixed outer ring reaches, because a chamber is which letter each wheel shows and not where in its own ring that letter sits — but the printed figure has a still ring, and this one does not.

And {{ chambers[2] }} pairs at the arity the scene's second setting offers. Both fall out of the nine letters and are computed rather than quoted, which matters more here than it looks: a third figure circulates alongside them, the number of compartments in the printed Tabula generalis, and this project has not checked it against a facsimile. So it is not on this page.

The alphabet

What makes the device a device is that the same letter names a different thing depending on which table you read it against. B is goodness, and difference, and utrum, and God, and justice, and avarice. That multiple assignment is not a convenience of notation — it is the mechanism, and it is why a figure needed its own representation in this library rather than fitting the Denckring's five slots.

The ninth question is Quomodo et cum quo — how, and with what. It is two of Llull's ten questions sharing one letter, and it is several words long, which is the reason the checker matches a spelled-out chamber by segmentation rather than by splitting on whitespace. Shortening it to Quomodo, as the shorter tables often do, makes the table tidier and the ninth rule wrong.

What it was said to be for, and against

Ratio quare facimus istam artem est ut ars magna facilius sciatur.

The reason we make this short art is so that the great Art may be learned more easily.

Ramon Llull, Ars brevis, prologue, 1308

… comme l'art de Lulle, à parler sans jugement de celles qu'on ignore.

… like the art of Lull: for speaking, without judgement, of the things one does not know.

René Descartes, Discours de la méthode II, 1637

… the most ignorant person … might write books in philosophy, poetry, politics, laws, mathematics, and theology, without the least assistance from genius or study.

Jonathan Swift, Gulliver's Travels III.5, 1726 — the engine at Lagado

Descartes' objection is the sharpest thing ever said about any machine on this stage, and it applies to all of them. A device that enumerates gives you sentences you have no grounds for. Harsdörffer's rings hand you a word and leave you to decide whether it is a word; Llull's wheels hand you a chamber and leave you to decide whether the argument it frames is sound. The difference is that a word's membership in German is decidable and an argument's soundness is not, which is exactly why one of these is checkable in this library and the other is only turnable.

Two things are worth keeping in view against the usual account. Eco's point is that the wheels do not freely generate new truths: the stock of principles is fixed, and heterodox combinations are excluded elsewhere in the Art, which cuts against the reading of the figure as an engine of open discovery. And the reading of it as a first computer is reception — a claim about how the twentieth century found it useful to see the object, not a finding about the object.

Put a question to the Art

Not a question answered — a chamber chosen. The figure reads letters out of the question, turns to a compartment of three distinct principles that contains them, and argues from the six concepts those three letters carry, and from nothing else.

Rows may contradict each other; Llull's own table has rows that go nowhere, and nihil is an answer here rather than a failure to give one. No overall conclusion comes back, because the device has never had one. That is Descartes' objection above, taken seriously rather than argued with: what you get is terms, and the judgement stays where it was.
