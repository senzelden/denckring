The machine

Hans Magnus Enzensberger described a poetry automaton in 1974, in Einladung zu einem Poesie-Automaten, and it was built for the Lyrik am Lech festival twenty-six years later. It stood on a square in Landsberg am Lech for three days. Press the button and an electromechanical split-flap display of the railway-platform kind clatters into a six-line poem.

The structure is the part that transfers, and it is very simple: {{ lines }} lines of {{ modules // lines }} modules, ten flaps on each. One press selects one flap per module.

10{{ exponent }} poems the board can show

That is not a rounded figure or an order of magnitude — it is exactly ten to the {{ exponent }}, because {{ modules }} modules of ten alternatives multiply to nothing else. The count on the scene is rendered as a power for that reason rather than as thirty-seven digits nobody reads.

Why the words here are not his

Enzensberger died in 2022. His word lists are in copyright until 2092, so none of them is in this repository and none ever will be. The {{ flaps }} flaps the shipped cartridge carries were written for this project, in the register the machine asks for — an industrial German of stations, concrete, wire and weather — and the credit on the scene says so in as many words rather than leaving a reader to assume otherwise.

What is Enzensberger's is the mechanism: six lines, six word-stores each, one grip into each compartment, and a poem that is one draw out of a space too large to exhaust. That is the thing the catalogue row describes and the thing the checker verifies. Put different words on the flaps and it is the same machine, which is the argument the second cartridge makes by existing.

The board is {{ columns }} columns wide, and that number is not a design choice either: the longest flap runs to {{ flap_cap }} characters, six of them sit side by side with five spaces between, and {{ columns }} is what that comes to. A board any narrower would have to hyphenate a word the machine printed whole.

The precedent it names

A device that holds a fixed inventory and draws one item per slot is Queneau's book with a motor in it, and the resemblance is not accidental — Enzensberger knew the Oulipo's work. What the Automat adds is that nobody has to turn anything. The strips in Queneau's book are lifted by a reader who chooses; the flaps here are dealt by a machine on a square, to whoever is standing in front of it. Both are the same wager the rings make, and the automaton is the version that has stopped pretending an author is present.
