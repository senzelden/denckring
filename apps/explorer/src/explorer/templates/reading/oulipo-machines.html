I

Cent mille milliards de poèmes

Ten sonnets, each printed on card cut into fourteen strips. Every sonnet keeps the same rhyme scheme and the same rhyme sounds, so line six of any sonnet will serve as line six of any other. Flip the strips and the book holds 1014 poems. Queneau's stated model was not the surrealists' exquisite corpse but a children's têtes folles flip-book, the kind cut into heads, bodies and legs.

The arithmetic is the smaller half of the trick. The hard part is writing 140 lines that are grammatically self-sufficient: no clause may reach across a strip, because the next strip is not going to be there. Every line has to end its own sentence or sit as an apposition that survives any neighbour. There is a second constraint that is easy to miss — no rhyme word may appear at two different positions, or a flip eventually lands the same word twice in one poem and the illusion of a written object collapses.

What this scene actually holds

Queneau died in 1976 and his ten sonnets are in copyright, so they are not shipped. What the strips carry was written for the scene, to his shape and at his scale: {{ positions }} positions of ten, {{ written }} lines, in English and in German.

They did not always. The strips carried three alternatives a position until this pass, which is 314, and the scene rendered that count — 4,782,969 — under an h1 reading Cent mille milliards. It was also laid out ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, which is an English sonnet and not the French one Queneau wrote. Both are fixed, and both were the same mistake: a machine described by the title of a different machine.

II

N+7

Take a text, take a dictionary, replace every noun with the {{ offset }}th noun that follows it. Nothing else changes — not the grammar, not the articles, not the line breaks. The result keeps the exact skeleton of the original and loses all of its subject matter, which is a fairly precise demonstration of how much of a sentence's meaning is carried by things that are not its nouns.

Jean Lescure proposed the method at one of the workshop's first meetings, on 13 February 1961; the write-up appears in La Littérature potentielle in 1973. The catalogue cites the second date because that is where the rule is set down, and the scene's own kicker cites the first because that is when it was invented. Both are right about different things.

The dictionary is not neutral

It is the other half of the constraint, and the half nobody names. A different word list is a different transformation on the same text, in the same way that a Denckring with different rings is a different device. So it is worth saying which one is running here:

The scene opens on the cat sat on the table, and cat lands on catacomb. It landed on catafalque until the migration to Open English WordNet moved the neighbours around. Nothing about the procedure changed; the dictionary underneath it did, and the output changed with it. A procedure is only as stable as the list it walks, and this is the one scene where that is a feature of the demonstration rather than a footnote to it.

Change the offset and the same text yields a different poem: N+7 is a family of transformations, not one of them. Seven is only the member the method is named after.

What the Oulipo added

Both machines make the same wager as Llull's wheels and Harsdörffer's discs three and six centuries earlier: that a rule applied mechanically to language will turn up something the operator could not have intended, and that the operator still has to decide which results are worth keeping. What the workshop added was candour about it. The constraint is published alongside the work, so the reader can run the machine too — which is the only reason this stage can exist.
